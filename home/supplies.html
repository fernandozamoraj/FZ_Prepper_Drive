Good to know

  • 1 gallon per person per day covers drinking (about half) and basic washing and cooking. Children, nursing mothers, sick people and hot weather need more. Pets: about an ounce per pound of body weight a day (a 50-lb dog drinks about 1/3 gallon); the calculator uses 1/2 gallon per pet.
  • Hidden water at home: the water heater tank (30–50 gallons: turn off its power or gas first, then drain from the bottom valve), the toilet tank (not the bowl), ice cubes and canned fruit and vegetable juices. Fill the bathtub and every pot when a storm is coming.
  • Make questionable water safe: let it settle and pour it through a cloth, then boil it 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft), or add 8 drops of 6% unscented bleach per gallon and wait 30 minutes; or use a filter (it doesn't stop viruses: add bleach too if sewage could be in it). See the EPA guide in PDF guides.
  • Don't ration water. Drink what you need today and look for more tomorrow.

Where to keep it

  • Cool, dark and dry (ideally 50–70°F). The living space beats the attic, garage and car: heat ruins food, medicine, batteries and water containers faster than anything. Under beds, closet floors and behind furniture work.
  • In labeled bins by job: "Power outage" (lights, radio, batteries, power banks), "Water", "First aid and medicine", "Cooking", "Sanitation". Clear plastic totes, labeled on the front and the top, so anyone can find things in the dark.
  • Spread it out: the main supply at home, a go-bag per person near the door, a kit in each car, a small kit at work or school. A fire or flood shouldn't take everything at once.
  • Up high if you can flood: nothing important on a basement floor; waterproof bins on shelves.
  • Fuel outside the living space: propane cylinders and tanks outdoors or in a detached shed, upright and shaded; gasoline in approved cans with stabilizer, away from the house, used within 6–12 months. Never fuel in the house, basement or an attached garage near living space.
  • Water: off bare concrete and away from chemicals (plastic absorbs fumes), out of the sun, with room to expand if it could freeze.
  • Keep it private. Don't advertise your supplies.

Keep it fresh

  • First in, first out: put new food, water and batteries behind the old ones and eat or use from the front. Write the purchase or expiry date in marker on everything.
  • Twice a year (when the clocks change): check dates on food, water, medicine and batteries; charge the power banks and power station; test the radios, CO and smoke alarms and flashlights; start the generator; update go-bag clothes for the season; refresh water you filled yourself (every 6 months).
  • Practice once a year: a "lights-out evening" or weekend with the power off. You'll find what's missing while it doesn't matter. Include starting this kit on the travel router with the internet unplugged.

Buying little by little

  • A little every grocery trip: one extra can, one pack of batteries, one gallon of water. In a few months the food and water phases are done without a big bill.
  • Buy before, not during: after a warning, shelves empty and prices jump. Buy heaters in summer and fans in winter, when they're on sale.
  • Sales-tax holidays: some states (Texas and Alabama, for example) have tax-free days for emergency supplies. Check yours.
  • Spend on what protects life (CO alarms, a genuine tourniquet, life jackets, an indoor-rated heater) and save money everywhere else. Used gear is fine for tarps, coolers and tools.
  • Use what you already own first: camping gear, the grill, the car, winter clothes.
  • Learn skills too, many free: first aid/CPR, Stop the Bleed, swimming, a ham license, CERT training from your local emergency management office.
